## Cross-Sample Common Structure

Most strong software brochures use some version of this order:

1. Product category and transformation promise.
2. Buyer-recognizable problem or market shift.
3. Product mechanism or platform loop.
4. Capability groups tied to workflows.
5. Role- or scenario-based value.
6. Trust: security, governance, scale, compliance, deployment, ecosystem.
7. Proof: customer names, metrics, quotes, analyst report, adoption figures.
8. Next step: demo, contact, trial, pilot, resource link.

## What Strong Brochures Do Well

- They explain the product category quickly.
- They start from the buyer's world, not the vendor's architecture.
- They group features by task, role, or scenario.
- They use simple pillars such as "easy, connected, trusted" or "secure, scalable, silo-free".
- They reserve technical proof for trust sections.
- They show integration/deployment as reassurance, not as the product story.
- They repeat one coherent promise instead of introducing a new slogan on every page.
- They make page titles meaningful enough to scan without reading body copy.
- They use diagrams for loops, maps, and before/after changes, not decorative complexity.
- They include customer evidence or measurable outcomes when available.

## Common Weaknesses

- Feature dump: long lists of product functions with no user journey.
- Category ambiguity: reader cannot tell whether it is CRM, review platform, AI tool, LMS, or meeting software.
- Technical overload: stack names and architecture details appear before buyer value.
- "AI washing": AI is mentioned as a label without explaining what decision or workflow improves.
- Weak proof: broad claims such as "industry-leading" without evidence.
- Unclear audience: brochure tries to speak to executives, admins, users, and engineers at once.
- Screenshot clutter: dense UI images require training to understand.
- Roadmap leakage: future ambitions presented as current product.
- Unexplained acronyms: platform seems sophisticated but opaque.

## Technical Detail Rule

Technical details belong in a brochure only when they reduce purchase risk. They should be translated into buyer-facing trust:

| Raw technical detail | Brochure translation |
|---|---|
| Spring Boot, Vue, FastAPI | Modular front-end/back-end architecture; supports independent deployment and service evolution. |
| LiveKit/WebRTC/Socket.IO | Real-time audio/video and collaboration capability. |
| MySQL/Redis/MinIO | Structured data persistence, session acceleration, and file/resource management. |
| Docker Compose/Nginx | Private deployment with unified gateway and service orchestration. |
| ASR/LLM/Qwen/VL model names | AI transcription, scoring, visual evidence analysis, and improvement suggestions. |
| API endpoints/database tables | Do not include; move to developer or deployment documentation. |
| Port numbers/passwords/config files | Never include in a brochure. |

## Brochure Quality Checklist

- Can a buyer explain the product in one sentence after 10 seconds?
- Does page 2 make the buyer feel "this is my problem"?
- Are capabilities grouped by user workflow rather than technical module?
- Are technical terms translated into business or operational value?
- Is every AI claim tied to a visible product output?
- Are current capabilities separated from roadmap/future vision?
- Are claims supportable from product evidence?
- Is there a clear buyer, evaluator, and end-user value path?
- Does the document end with a realistic next step?
